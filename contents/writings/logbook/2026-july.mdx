Verticalizing on a subject to its deepest levels used to be reserved for very few passionate, skilled people, especially in scientific disciplines. With the advent of LLMs, the paradigm seems to be shifting toward flatness (what some call "slop"), reaching a point where we've simply forgotten to think. I've spent the last month thinking about how this concept is evolving and changing during this AI era.

Now that horizontality has become a commodity, could verticality become one too? We're told specialization and experience are the edge, but what if models become better at going deep than we can ever be? What if they can do that without us steering the wheel or having a meaningful effect through our context? This translates to something concrete: will there be a moment when mathematics written by an LLM is no longer comprehensible to mathematicians? I keep thinking about proofs so dense, so optimized, so alien that the humans who built them can't verify them. We'd trust them, execute them, rely on them—but suppose we cannot understand them. Are we going to call this still mathematics, or something else entirely?

This kind of reasoning applies to other disciplines too, even to material objects. Will we reach a point where hardware designs are only comprehensible to machines? Chip layouts, thermal models, material combinations too complex for any human brain to hold. We'd deploy systems we couldn't reason about, couldn't debug, couldn't improve except by asking other systems.

And finally: will there be a moment when it's no longer necessary to think of the LLM as a tool, but as the end itself? When we will stop directing intelligence and just start implicitly harvesting it? Basically, when the goal is going to become just feeding the machine and not understanding its output?

I don't have answers to any of these. Some feel like science fiction, others feel like inevitabilities. What I do know is this: for now, I'm still responsible for what I build. I still need to understand the code I ship, the arguments I make, the systems I design. I still need to be a professional software engineer who takes decision, use LLMs as tools (without competing with them) and is deeply passionate about many different topics! Until that changes, going deep is the only way to stay one step above the global anesthesia that is spreading throughout the world. But I'm watching—and honestly, I'm not sure I'll recognize the moment everything flips.
